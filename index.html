Gambler's Fallacy
✓ Done
●●●●○ High
▼
The belief that past random events influence future ones. A coin has no memory. Probability resets every single flip. Watch how quickly your brain stops believing that.
▸ Watch the sequence build, then predict

⚡ The Trap Sprung
The correct answer is always 50/50. A coin has no memory of previous flips. The probability of heads is 0.5 regardless of whether you just saw 10 heads in a row. Casinos are built on the people who believe a cold slot machine is "due."

Sports streaks: "He's missed 5 free throws in a row, he's definitely hitting this one" — no. Each shot is its own independent event.
⚡ Used In The Wild
Gambling industry design, sports betting, day trading ("the stock has been down 5 days — it must bounce"), and lottery marketing all exploit this. The casino's "recent results" display is pure manipulation.

05
Conjunction Fallacy
✓ Done
●●●●○ High
▼
A specific scenario feels more probable than a general one — even though a specific scenario is always mathematically less likely. Narrative coherence hijacks probability.
▸ Read carefully, then answer
Linda is 31, single, outspoken, and very bright. She majored in philosophy. As a student she was deeply concerned with discrimination and social justice, and participated in anti-nuclear demonstrations.

Which is more probable?
⚡ The Trap Sprung
A is always more probable. The probability of two things being true simultaneously (bank teller AND feminist) can never exceed the probability of just one of them being true. The set of "bank tellers" contains all bank tellers — including feminist ones. Option B is a subset of Option A. It cannot be more likely. Yet 85% of people in Tversky & Kahneman's original study chose B. The vivid backstory made B feel coherent — and coherence masquerades as probability.
Real-World Examples
Legal narratives: Juries find specific, detailed accounts of crimes more believable than vague ones — even though specificity mathematically reduces probability. A compelling story is not evidence.
Conspiracy theories: A specific theory with named actors and detailed mechanisms feels more credible than a vague one, even though each added specific claim makes the whole thing less probable.
Investment pitches: "Tech stocks will rise" vs "Tech stocks in AI healthcare will rise 40% by Q3." The specific version feels more researched and credible. It's less likely to be exactly right.
⚡ Used In The Wild
Political messaging, legal storytelling, marketing narratives, and horoscopes all exploit this. A specific, detailed prediction feels more accurate than a vague one — which is why pseudoscience loves specificity. It signals knowledge without requiring accuracy.

06
Sunk Cost Trap
✓ Done
●●●●● Critical
▼
You continue investing in something because of what you've already spent — not because of future value. Past costs that can't be recovered should never influence future decisions. They always do.
⚡ The Trap Sprung
Real-World Examples
Concorde fallacy: The British and French governments continued funding the Concorde project for years after it was clear it would never be commercially viable — because they had already spent so much. The term "Concorde fallacy" is now synonymous with sunk cost reasoning.
Bad relationships: "We've been together 8 years, I can't leave now." The 8 years are gone. The only relevant question is whether continuing is worth it going forward.
Eating when full: "I paid for it, I'm finishing it" — the money is spent regardless. Eating when full adds cost (discomfort) rather than recovering the sunk cost.
⚡ Used In The Wild
Free trials that require credit cards, loyalty programs, gym memberships, and timeshare sales all exploit sunk cost psychology. Once you've invested time, money, or identity into something, walking away feels like losing — even when staying is the actual loss.

Misleading Averages
✓ Done
●●●●○ High
▼
Mean, median, and mode are all technically "averages." Which one you report changes the story entirely. A company with one billionaire CEO and 99 minimum-wage workers has an "average salary" of over $100,000.
▸ A company reports average salaries. Which "average" would they advertise?
Annual salaries at MegaCorp Inc:
CEO: $4,200,000  |  3 VPs: $280,000 each
12 Managers: $95,000 each  |  84 Staff: $38,000 each
⚡ The Trap Sprung
The mean ($98,400) is technically accurate — but it's dragged upward by the CEO's salary. 84 out of 100 employees earn $38,000, which is both the median and the mode. A job posting saying "average salary $98,400" is not lying — it's choosing the average that serves the narrative. This is one of the most common and legal statistical manipulations in existence.
Real-World Examples
Wealth statistics: "Average American net worth" sounds impressive until you realize the median is roughly one-fifth of the mean, because a handful of billionaires distort the arithmetic average upward for everyone.
University salary claims: Schools advertise "average graduate starting salary" using mean, which is inflated by a small number of high earners in finance and tech. Most graduates earn much less.
Real estate: "Average home price in the neighborhood" can hide that 90% of homes are affordable and 2 mansions are dragging the number up dramatically.
⚡ Used In The Wild
Recruitment advertising, income inequality debates, university marketing, and housing market reporting all choose their "average" strategically. Always ask: mean, median, or mode? And how many outliers are distorting it?

Correlation ≠ Causation
✓ Done
●●●●● Critical
▼
Two things moving together does not mean one causes the other. Real correlations between obviously unrelated things are everywhere — and they look exactly like real evidence.
▸ This correlation is real. r = 0.67. What does it prove?
Nicolas Cage films released per year vs. Swimming pool drownings (USA, 1999–2009)

Correlation coefficient r = 0.67 — considered a strong correlation in many social sciences
⚡ The Trap Sprung
The honest answer is coincidence — and the more important lesson is that r=0.67 proves nothing about causation regardless. In a dataset of thousands of variables tracked over time, spurious correlations are statistically inevitable. The Cage/drowning correlation is from Tyler Vigen's "Spurious Correlations" project, which documents hundreds of real, high-correlation relationships between completely unrelated variables. Correlation is a starting point for investigation — never a conclusion.
Real-World Examples
Ice cream and drownings: Both increase in summer. Ice cream doesn't cause drownings. Summer is the confounding variable. Confounders are everywhere in real research.
Shoe size and reading ability: In children, both correlate strongly. Older children have bigger feet and read better. Age is the confounder. Neither causes the other.
Social media and depression: Genuinely correlated in studies. But does social media cause depression, or do depressed people use more social media? Or does a third factor cause both? This is an active research debate.
⚡ Used In The Wild
Supplement advertising ("people who take X live longer"), nutrition science media coverage, social science policy claims, and anti-vaccine arguments all present correlations as causal evidence. Always ask: what's the mechanism? What are the confounders? Has this been replicated with controls?

Sample Size Illusion
✓ Done
●●●○○ Moderate
▼
"4 out of 5 dentists recommend..." sounds like overwhelming consensus. What if it was 4 out of 5 dentists total? Small samples produce extreme statistics that look identical to large-sample results.
▸ Rate how convincing each claim sounds
⚡ The Trap Sprung
The claims sound different because we project sample size onto the percentage — but none of the claims above disclosed their sample size. "4 out of 5 dentists" in the famous Colgate ad was based on a mailed survey to a small, self-selected group. Small samples produce extreme results by chance alone. A drug that works for 3 out of 4 patients in a trial of 4 is far less meaningful than one that works for 75% of 10,000 patients. The number always matters as much as the percentage.
Real-World Examples
"9 out of 10 users preferred...": In product tests, small panels of 10 people are common. One person's preference flips the result from 80% to 90%. These tests are often not published if the result is unfavorable — only the winning one is advertised.
Early clinical trials: Phase I drug trials often involve fewer than 30 patients. Promising results vanish in Phase III trials of thousands. The small sample was noise.
Online reviews: A product with five 5-star reviews is not more trustworthy than one with 500 reviews averaging 4.2 stars. Sample size swamps individual ratings.
⚡ Used In The Wild
Product advertising, supplement claims, political polling reporting ("leading among likely voters!"), and medical news coverage all suppress sample size information. The default question to any statistic: how many people, selected how?

Percent vs Percentage Points
✓ Done
●●●●○ High
▼
"Crime rose 100%" and "crime rose 1 percentage point" can describe the exact same change. Relative vs absolute framing produces wildly different emotional impact from identical data.
▸ Which headline sounds more alarming?
⚡ The Trap Sprung
If crime affects 1% of residents and rises to 2%, that is both "crime doubled" (100% relative increase) and "a 1 percentage point rise" (absolute change). Both are true. One sounds like a crisis; one sounds like a rounding error. Relative figures amplify small changes — useful for drug efficacy claims ("reduces risk by 50%!"), political fear campaigns, and any situation where the absolute baseline is small. Always ask: 50% of what?
Real-World Examples
Drug advertising: "Reduces heart attack risk by 36%!" — the fine print shows absolute risk went from 1.4% to 0.9%. The relative figure is technically accurate and sounds far more impressive than "reduces risk by 0.5 percentage points."
Tax cuts: "We cut your tax rate by 20%!" could mean a rate went from 25% to 20% (5 percentage points) or from 25% to 5% (20 percentage points). These are very different outcomes with identical wording.
Investment returns: A fund that returned 2% when cash returned 1% "doubled the benchmark" — but the absolute advantage is 1 percentage point, which may not justify the fees.
⚡ Used In The Wild
Pharmaceutical ads, political campaign claims, financial marketing, and crime reporting all strategically choose between relative and absolute figures. The rule: when the base rate is small, relative figures inflate impact. Always find the absolute number.

●●●●● Critical
▼
Select only the data points that support your conclusion and the trend looks ironclad. Reveal the hidden data and the trend evaporates or reverses. This is how most pseudoscience "studies" work.

⚡ Hidden Data Revealed

⚡ Used In The Wild

Hot Hand Fallacy
✓ Done
●●●○○ Moderate
▼
The belief that a person on a streak is "hot" and more likely to succeed again. In most domains this is statistically false — but it feels undeniably true when you watch it happen.
▸ A basketball player has made 5 shots in a row. What are the odds they make the next one?
⚡ The Research Says
This is genuinely contested. The original Gilovich et al. (1985) study found no statistical evidence for hot hands in basketball — each shot was independent of previous shots. However, a 2016 reanalysis found a small but real hot hand effect once selection bias was corrected for. The consensus: any real effect is far smaller than perceived. The feeling of a hot hand is a cognitive illusion; the statistical reality is debated but modest at best. Meanwhile, defenders adjust to hot shooters — which may wash out the effect entirely.
Real-World Examples
Day trading: Traders who have a good week believe they have "found their rhythm" and increase position sizes. The next week regresses to the mean — now with larger losses.
Slot machines: A player who wins twice in a row feels "on a roll." The machine has no memory. Each pull is independent. The hot hand feeling is an artifact of pattern detection in noise.
Investment funds: Funds that outperform for 3 years attract massive inflows as investors chase the "hot" manager. Most subsequent years show regression to market average performance.
⚡ Used In The Wild
Sports betting lines, investment marketing ("top performing fund!"), and casino psychology all exploit hot hand perception. The feeling of momentum is real. The statistical basis for it usually isn't.

②

⑤

⑥
Second Urgency Layer — Cart Expiry. A second timer compounds the pressure. Multiple urgency signals stack — each one independently adds friction to "wait and think." Pattern: Artificial Urgency.
⑦
Trust Signals — Guarantee + Reviews. The money-back guarantee links to terms that make it nearly impossible to claim. The 4.9/5 rating from 14,203 reviews cannot be verified. Both exploit authority and social proof to lower skepticism. Pattern: Authority Spoofing + Social Proof Faking.

Pretexting — The Social Engineering Playbook
✓ Done
●●●●● Critical
▼
Pretexting is creating a fabricated scenario to manipulate someone into revealing information or taking action. Every social engineering attack follows a predictable sequence. Learn the sequence and it becomes visible every time.
You are an employee at a company. Your phone rings. Walk through the scenario and make decisions.
⚡ The Full Playbook — Regardless of Your Choices
Every social engineering call follows the same four-phase structure:

① Establish Authority — Name, role, organization. Make you feel they have power over you or can help you. Often includes insider-sounding details (your name, your company's IT system name) obtained from LinkedIn or company websites.

② Create Urgency — A problem exists RIGHT NOW that requires immediate action. Urgency prevents you from pausing to verify. "Your account is being accessed as we speak."

③ Isolate from Verification — "Don't call the main IT line, they'll just create a ticket." "Don't tell your manager yet." Removing your ability to check with others removes your safety net.

④ Extract — The actual ask: credentials, a wire transfer, gift cards, access to a system. By this point you've already agreed to three smaller things (sunk cost) and feel you're solving a problem, not being robbed.

The defense: Hang up. Call back on a number you find independently. Verify before you act. No legitimate IT or security team will pressure you not to verify.

Verification Habits
How to check any claim, identity, or offer
▼
Verification is the universal antidote. Every manipulation pattern in this guide — scams, social engineering, gaslighting, authority exploitation — loses power the moment you independently verify a claim before acting on it. These habits take seconds and prevent most manipulation from working.
When someone claims... Verify by... Red flag if...
They're from your bank / IRS / Microsoft Hang up. Call the official number from the institution's website or your card — not the number they gave you They tell you not to call the main line, or insist on continuing the current call
An email is from a legitimate company Check the full sender domain (not just the display name). Type the company URL directly into your browser — don't click links in the email Domain has subtle misspellings, extra words, or wrong TLD (.net instead of .com)
A website is legitimate Read the actual domain — the part immediately before .com/.org/etc. Ignore everything before it. Check HTTPS. Search the domain on whois.domaintools.com Brand name appears as a subdomain (paypal.com.evil.ru) or with character substitutions (paypa1.com)

The Pause Protocol
Every manipulation technique in this guide is defeated by a single habit: creating time between a request and your response.

When pressure, urgency, or fear is present — pause. The pressure itself is a signal. Legitimate institutions, genuine offers, and honest people do not require you to act before you can think. Any situation that cannot survive a 24-hour pause deserves that pause most of all.

"I need to think about this. I'll get back to you." — this sentence ends most manipulation attempts. Practice saying it without justification.

If You've Been Targeted
If you responded to a scam or gave information: Contact your bank immediately if financial information was shared. Place a fraud alert at one of the three credit bureaus (Equifax, Experian, TransUnion) — they're required to notify the others. Change passwords on any accounts that use the same credentials. File a report with the FTC and IC3 regardless of the amount — reports build the databases that protect others.

If you sent money: Contact your bank or the payment method immediately. Gift card payments: call the issuer's fraud line — recovery is rare but possible. Wire transfers: contact your bank within 24–48 hours, request a recall. Crypto transfers: essentially unrecoverable — document everything for law enforcement reporting.

Being targeted is not a failure of intelligence. These techniques work because they exploit fundamental cognitive systems, not ignorance. Experts, security professionals, and executives are targeted and caught by social engineering regularly. The patterns in this guide exist because they work on most people most of the time.
